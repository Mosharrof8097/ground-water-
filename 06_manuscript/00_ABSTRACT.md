Monitoring trace metals in groundwater requires laboratory instrumentation that
is scarcest where contamination is most widespread. Whether parameters a field
team can measure can stand in for that analysis has not been tested under
validation accounting for spatial structure, left censoring and transfer. Using
271 wells of the BGS and DPHE National Hydrochemical Survey of Bangladesh, with
external validation on 1,027 wells in fifteen unseen districts, we screened
thirteen trace metals across five predictor tiers ordered by cost. Six metals
proved unmodellable because the laboratory reported a detection limit rather
than a measurement in up to 97 % of wells. Censoring also works in the opposite
direction: an analysis that ignores the censoring flag classifies
65 wells as nitrate reducing where only 2
meet the criteria, and reports log-scale predictive skill that disappears when
scoring is restricted to measured wells. Spatial blocking cut feasible target
and tier combinations from 23 to 10; nickel,
rated at R² = +0.288 under random folds, fell to
-0.204. On unseen districts no model retained concentration
skill, but exceedance ranking transferred, and the fitted model was not what
carried it. A single dissolved iron measurement ranked wells better than the
model built on iron, manganese and depth, 0.891 against
0.828 (1.8e-08), missing fewer contaminated wells at every
laboratory burden, while on the development wells the ordering reversed
(0.867 against 0.742). Split conformal intervals held
89.8 % to 91.7 % coverage locally but 72.7 %
to 99.0 % on transfer. Surrogate screening orders wells usefully
without quantifying them, and the fitted model earns nothing over the cheapest
measurement it contains.

**Keywords:** groundwater; trace metals; left-censored data; spatial cross
validation; conformal prediction; Bangladesh
